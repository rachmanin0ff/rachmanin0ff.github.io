Method

Seven measurements taken from the sound alone

Two sources, kept apart. The sound gives loudness and attack times: a 2,048-point Fourier transform sliding at 23.44 frames a second, 5,974 windows, attacks picked from spectral flux. The notes come from the score — a Duo-Art piano-roll transcription of Op. 3 № 2, 1,710 notes, aligned to this performance by dynamic time warping and then snapped to its detected attacks; 79 % of its chords land within 100 ms of a real one. Nothing on this page infers a pitch from a spectrum, so no phantom harmony can appear. Every plate is live: drag it to move the music, and the MOTIF buttons step through the twenty places where the bell figure — A, G♯, C♯ — returns in the lowest voice.

01  /  Proportion

The golden section keeps its appointment

The prelude sounds for 244.82 seconds. Cut that span in golden proportion and the knife falls at 151.60. The most violent attack in the performance — the largest single-frame rise in spectral energy anywhere in four minutes — lands at 150.06. The error is 1.54 seconds: six-tenths of one percent of the piece.

The lower golden section, 93.82 seconds, is the stranger one, because there is nothing there. It is the emptiest ground in the work — the Lento spending its last breath before the Agitato — and the nearest boundary the harmony itself declares, measured by chroma novelty, sits at 93.2. The piece is loudest at one golden section and faintest at the other. Neither proportion is written in the score. Both are enacted in the playing.

02  /  Tonal centre

The tonic will not share

Add up how long each of the twelve pitches actually sounds and the prelude turns out to be a monarchy. C♯ holds 29.3 % of all sounding time — half again as much as the dominant G♯ at 19.7 %, and more than the bottom seven pitches put together, which manage 21.1 % between them. Nothing else comes near it.

The bottom of the list is the stranger end. The seven notes of C♯ minor take 90.7 % of the sounding time; the five chromatic outsiders share the remaining 9.3 % between them. F — which is really E♯, the note that would turn this minor into a major — sounds for 0.20 % of the piece: eleven notes out of 1,710. For all its reputation as chromatic music, the prelude keeps almost entirely inside its own scale and gets its darkness from weight rather than from strange notes.

03  /  Information

What the sound says, and what the notes say

Treat each window as a probability distribution over twelve pitch classes and take its Shannon entropy. All twelve equally present reads 3.585 bits; a pure C♯ minor scale reads 2.807. Measured from the notes, this prelude averages 2.606 bits over ten-second windows — 72.7 % of the ceiling, and below the seven-note line. Its harmony is narrower than the scale it is written in, because the tonic takes so much of the room.

This is worth saying plainly, because measuring the same quantity from the audio spectrum instead gives 3.478 bits — 97 % saturation, a piece drowning in chromaticism. That reading is wrong. A piano string radiates a whole harmonic series, so a spectrum hears fifths and thirds nobody played, and every window fills up with pitches that are not in the score. The gap between 3.478 and 2.606 is the exact size of that illusion.

04  /  Self-similarity

Where the piece rhymes exactly

Take every half-second of the piece and compare its pitch-class content with every other by the angle between the two vectors. The bright diagonal is the trivial truth that a moment resembles itself. Everything off it is memory. The ribbons along the top and left edges count how many pitch classes are sounding, and the pale ticks mark the windows that hold only one.

The bright blocks come from thinness more than from repetition. Of the 490 half-second windows, 120 — a quarter of the piece — hold exactly one pitch class, and 106 of those 120 land on A, G♯ or C♯: the three notes of the opening bell. Two unisons on the same pitch match perfectly whatever surrounds them, so the prelude rhymes most exactly at the moments when it has least to say.

Measured block by block, the ranking runs against intuition. The section that rhymes with itself most is the coda, at a mean off-diagonal similarity of 0.561, because it says one thing over and over: 29 of its 34 unison windows are C♯. Second is the Agitato at 0.524 — a continuous chordal weave six pitch classes wide, where every bar overlaps its neighbours, and where not one window falls to a single pitch. Last, at 0.307, is the Lento. It alternates between bare octaves on A, on G♯ and on C♯, and those three vectors are mutually orthogonal: a moment of pure A has nothing whatever in common with a moment of pure G♯.

So the two ends of the piece do answer each other, and the plot shows why. The coda's similarity to the opening is 0.481, against 0.418 for the opening and the Agitato — a rhyme carried not by a returning theme but by a returning texture. Both ends empty out onto the same three pitches, and the bright corner four minutes from the start is the sound of the piece arriving back at bare octaves, having spent everything in between filling the space between them.

05  /  Rhythm

How the climax is actually built

Two numbers per section, both straight off the note list. The gap between attacks: 0.896 s in the Lento, 0.256 in the Agitato, 0.244 through the culmination, 0.640 in Tempo I, 1.834 in the coda. And the notes struck per attack: 4.58, then 2.66, then 4.83, then 7.16, then 5.09.

Read them together and the architecture gives itself away. The Agitato runs at three and a half times the opening speed on the thinnest texture in the piece — two bare voices. The culmination keeps that speed and doubles the weight of every attack. Then Tempo I, the loudest music in the work, drops back to two and a half times slower than the Agitato and puts seven notes under every hammer-blow. The catastrophe is assembled by adding hands, and the tempo falls away as the weight arrives.

06  /  Register

Six octaves, and a bell at the bottom of all of them

The whole aligned score, drawn as a piano roll: time across, pitch up, one bar per note, coloured by distance from the tonic and shaded by how hard it was struck. The five notes outside C♯ minor are drawn outlined and a little taller — 235 bars out of 1,710, and you can count them.

The mean sounding pitch tells the arc: MIDI 59.5 in the Lento, 60.9 in the Agitato, 66.2 at the culmination — half an octave of lift — then 66.1 through Tempo I, and 57.0 in the coda. The piece ends lower than it began. And along the floor of the plot the same note keeps returning: C♯1, the lowest on the roll, struck twenty-two times between the sixth second and the two-hundred-and-thirty-fifth. Those strikes are spread unevenly rather than held as a pedal: eight fall in the Lento, only one in the whole Agitato, which goes forty-four seconds without the note at all. Six octaves get built on top of that note and taken away again, and it is always there to come back to.

07  /  Voice-leading

Violence built from the smoothest moves available

Now that the notes are real, so are the chords: each of the 399 attacks is named from the pitches actually struck and their actual bass. The prelude turns out to live in a small room. The fourteen chords on this wheel carry 47.6 % of its 399 attacks, and the top three alone — C♯ in bare octaves, G♯ in bare octaves, and C♯ minor over E — carry nearly a fifth of the piece on their own.

The heaviest single edge is G♯ tipping into the diminished chord over D♯, taken ten times; then A into D⁷, and D⁷ back to G♯, eight each. All three sit in the dominant region, so the piece spends its time handing tension along rather than letting it resolve. That is the paradox in one image: traffic this local, sounding like catastrophe. What makes it enormous is the weight of each chord rather than the distance between them.

08  /  Return

The bell comes back faster, and then stops coming back

The opening figure — A, G♯, C♯ — can be found exactly, because the score is exact: search the lowest sounding voice for that succession and it turns up twenty times. Their spacing is the interesting part. Across the first half of the piece the statements sit a median 9.3 seconds apart; across the second half, 5.5. Fitted against elapsed time the gap shrinks by 0.054 seconds for every second that passes, so the bell tolls at a quickening rate through the whole span — an accelerando operating on the form rather than on the beat.

Then it stops. The twentieth statement falls at 184.7 seconds and there is not another one: the last 60.5 seconds, a quarter of the piece, carry none. What survives into the coda is the figure's final note by itself — twenty of the coda's twenty-five single-pitch-class windows are C♯. The bell accelerates towards the climax, and afterwards only its resting note is left ringing.

The histogram underneath answers a different question: where the bass goes between one attack and the next. Motion by a third accounts for 42.7 % of the 398 moves, against 18.8 % for fifths and fourths. For a piece whose reputation rests on dominant-to-tonic weight, the bass line spends most of its life sliding by thirds.
